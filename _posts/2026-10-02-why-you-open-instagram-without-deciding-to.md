You pick up your phone to do one thing. Check a message. Look something up. Set an alarm.

Instagram is open instead.

Not because you decided to open it. You did not decide anything. Your thumb went somewhere it has been a thousand times, and by the time you noticed, you were already three posts in.

That is annoying. It is also the most ordinary thing your brain does, and there is a decent amount of research explaining exactly why.

## It happens to me, and knowing why does not stop it

I am not writing this from the outside. I do the same thing.

I come home after a work day with a plan for the evening, and I find myself scrolling through Instagram as if the plan had never existed. Sometimes I wake up out of it. I notice what I am doing, I see the time, and I put the phone down.

And a lot of the time I notice, and stay anyway.

That second part bothered me more than the first. Not knowing is one thing. Knowing, and continuing, is something else. It made me feel like the decision was not really mine.

Sometimes a friend sends me one short video and I watch a dozen more. Sometimes I am looking for something without admitting what — after my engagement ended, I know exactly whose name I was hoping to see. Sometimes I just go with the flow instead of doing the harder thing I said I would do.

## It is a routine, and that is a technical claim

My own theory was simple: we built a routine. Phone comes out, these apps get opened, something easy arrives. It turns out that is close to what the research says, and the research is more specific about the mechanism than I was.

[Neal, Wood, Labrecque and Lally (2012)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jesp.2011.10.011) tested what actually sets a habit off. Strong habits, they found, are triggered by the context — the place, the situation, the cue that was there the last hundred times — and are relatively unaffected by your current goals. The cue fires the response. Your goal is not what moves your hand.

The finding that stayed with me is the second one. People believe their habits are goal-driven. Asked why they did something, they produce a reason. The reason feels true. It is mostly constructed after the fact.

So, when I tell myself I opened Instagram because I was bored, or because I wanted to see if anyone had replied — that explanation arrives after my thumb has already moved.

The clearest case is when the goal is real. I open the app to send a friend a message. That is a genuine reason, and I do send the message. Then I am still there eleven minutes later, and nothing about those eleven minutes was decided. The goal got me in. The habit kept me.

[Oulasvirta and colleagues (2012)](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00779-011-0412-2) named the phone version of this: the checking habit. Brief, repetitive inspections of whatever might have changed. Not decided each time. Learned by repetition, like everything else your hands do without you.

## Why you do not notice yourself doing it

[Wood, Quinn and Kashy (2002)](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.83.6.1281) ran diary studies where people reported what they were doing and thinking at intervals through the day. When the behaviour was habitual — performed almost daily, in the same place — people were usually thinking about something unrelated to it. The action does not need your attention, so it does not get any.

Which is why you cannot count them. In a small study of 23 people over two weeks, [Andrews and colleagues (2015)](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0139004) compared what people estimated about their phone use against what their phones actually recorded. Estimates of time were roughly reasonable. Estimates of how many times a day they picked up the phone did not correlate with reality at all.

You have a sense of how long you spend. You have no idea how often you reach.

## Noticing is not the same as stopping

The part of my own experience I understood least — noticing and staying anyway — has a fairly direct answer. [Neal, Wood, Wu and Kurlander (2011)](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0146167211419863) gave cinemagoers popcorn. Some of it was fresh. Some of it was a week old.

People with a strong cinema-popcorn habit ate the same amount of both. Not because they liked stale popcorn. Their hunger barely mattered and their enjoyment barely mattered. They were in the place where they always eat popcorn, so they ate popcorn.

Two details make this more than a funny result.

It only happened in the cinema. Given the same stale popcorn in a meeting room, the same people ate far less — the habit was tied to the context, not to the food.

And it stopped when they had to eat with their non-dominant hand. Not because that made them think harder about nutrition. Because it interrupted the automatic execution of a movement they had made a thousand times.

Nothing about that intervention involves willpower. It makes the action slightly less automatic, and the behaviour changes.

## Where I have to be careful

There is a version of this article, and you have read it before, which says that habit simply overrides intention: your willpower is no match for your wiring, science has proven it, buy this thing.

That claim is not as well supported as it sounds. [Gardner, Lally and Rebar (2020)](https://doi.org/10.1111/spc3.12553) reviewed 52 studies testing exactly it — whether strong habit weakens the link between what you intend and what you do. Thirteen supported it. Nine found no effect. Five were mixed. Four found the opposite.

So, I am not going to tell you your intentions are powerless. The evidence does not say that.

What it does say is narrower and, I think, more useful: the thing that sets the behaviour off is not your goal. You can want, sincerely, to not open Instagram, and that wanting is simply not what the cue is connected to.

## And the feed is not sitting still

All of the above would be true of a habit with nothing behind it. Instagram is not that.

[Bayer, Anderson and Tokunaga (2022)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2352250X22000057) describe social media habits as associations learned between cues and responses when the response is rewarded — and the cues are both external, like a notification, and internal, like boredom. The reward is not reliable, which is the point. Sometimes there is nothing. Sometimes there is exactly the thing you wanted.

So, the feed keeps offering things you might want. Football highlights. A scene from a film you liked. Music. Tips and tricks. Makeup. Slices of somebody’s afternoon.

And it learns. Break up with someone, look at one post about it, and now your feed is breakup posts, and you cannot make it stop. I am still working on not looking at those. Sometimes I cannot help it.

Even good content does this. Too much Christian content became a problem for me in a way I did not expect: reading about God is not the same as spending that time with Him, and the feed is very happy to let you confuse the two.

Then there are the new surfaces. Threads. Notes. Whatever ships next quarter. More invitations, more places for something to have changed since you last looked.

And once you are inside, it drifts. I check one thing and surface somewhere with no relation to it — a subject I did not know I was curious about, a person I have never met, a corner of the app I did not choose. Each step is small and reasonable. The distance from where you started is not.

That is the part that made me want to do something about it. Not the time exactly. The feeling of having spent an evening moving through a maze somebody else built, taking whichever turn was offered.

## How long this took to build

If it feels like this habit has been there forever, it has not. [Lally and colleagues (2010)](https://doi.org/10.1002/ejsp.674) followed people forming new daily habits and found a median of about 66 days to reach automaticity — not the 21 days that gets repeated everywhere.

Two months of picking up your phone in the same situations. That is all it takes to build something you now experience as involuntary.

## So what actually helps

If the trigger is not your goal, then trying harder at the goal is aiming at the wrong thing. That is why time limits did nothing for me. I dismissed them without reading them. They arrived after the decision, addressed to a person who was not making one.

And blocking has its own problem. An app that stands between you and something you want becomes an opponent, and you win that fight by deleting the app. Then it helps you never again.

What the popcorn study points at is smaller and stranger: interrupt the execution. Put something between the cue and the completed movement. Not a wall. A moment.

Enough of one that the action stops being automatic, and you get to do the thing you thought you were doing all along — choose.

You might still open Instagram. That is genuinely fine. The difference is whether you opened it or it opened.
