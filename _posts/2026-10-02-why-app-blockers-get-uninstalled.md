Everybody who has used one knows the moment. Not the day you install it — that day feels great. The day you remove it.

There is usually a reason available. You need the app for work. The limit is set wrong. You will put it back next week.

I have given all three. Here is what actually happened.

## It worked, and then it did not

This was years ago now. The last blocker I used was the one built into my Xiaomi phone, and I kept it for months, and it worked. YouTube went from four or five hours a day to about one. Facebook went from one or two hours to thirty minutes. That is not a small change and I am not going to pretend it was.

Then a bad stretch arrived — nothing dramatic, just the ordinary kind — and I went back to social media to put the evenings somewhere.

I did not uninstall the blocker. That is the part I want to be precise about, because it is the part I misunderstood for a long time. I negotiated with it.

Thirty minutes of YouTube became an hour. An hour became an hour and a half. An hour and a half became two.

And I had a reason each time, a good one. I was watching motivational videos. Things that would help me. Thirty minutes is not enough time to actually learn anything — which is true, and which was also not why I was doing it.

I held two hours for about a week. Then I removed the blocker altogether, and I was back where I started.

## The explanation I reached for was the weakest one available

For a long time, I explained this to myself in one sentence: my willpower ran out.

It is a satisfying sentence. It has a villain, and the villain is me, which at least feels honest.

It is also the explanation with the least evidence behind it. The idea that self-control is a resource that depletes with use was tested by [23 laboratories running the same preregistered protocol on 2,141 people](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1745691616652873). The combined effect was 0.04, with a confidence interval that included zero. Whatever was happening to me over those weeks, a tank running empty is probably not it.

So, if it was not willpower draining away, what was it?

## It turns out I was following a script

Kovacs, Wu and Bernstein studied this directly. They run HabitLab, a tool that lets people pick how aggressively they want to be interrupted while browsing, and they looked at the logs of [more than 8,000 users](https://arxiv.org/abs/2101.11743).

People start on the hard settings. Over time they slide to easier and easier ones.

That is not the interesting part. This is: while sliding, they keep declining to make the easier setting permanent. Asked whether to remember the choice, they say no — ask me again next time. They are still planning to go back to the hard version. They just are not going back today.

Thirty minutes to an hour to an hour and a half to two. Ask me again tomorrow.

I thought I was failing privately. I was tracing a curve that eight thousand other people had already traced.

## The escape hatch is part of the design

The same group found something that should worry anyone building these tools. In three field experiments, they compared rotating between different interventions against keeping one. Rotating [worked better and got uninstalled more](https://doi.org/10.1145/3274364). Effectiveness and tolerability pulled in opposite directions.

That is the whole shape of the problem, and my Xiaomi story is one instance of it.

A blocker you can edit is a blocker you will edit, because editing it is always easier than obeying it. The escape hatch is not a flaw somebody forgot to close — it is there so the tool is bearable, and it is the exact mechanism by which the tool stops working.

And a blocker you cannot edit does not solve this. It just moves the decision. Now the only lever left is the uninstall button, so that is the lever you pull.

## Why the removal happens when it does

There is a name for the thing that makes a restriction feel like an opponent. [Psychological reactance](https://doi.org/10.1027/2151-2604/a000222) is the motivation to restore a freedom when something threatens it. The restriction does not just fail to persuade you. It generates pressure in the opposite direction, aimed at the restriction itself.

Which explains the timing. I did not remove the blocker during a good stretch. I removed it during a bad one.

When things are fine, a limit is a mild inconvenience you agreed to. When you are already struggling, the same limit stands between you and the easiest relief available — and getting rid of it is not weakness. It is the most direct way to take something back on a day when very little is going your way.

The blocker was not built to be a problem. But when I needed it most, it had become one, and I solved it.

## Where I have to be careful

The easy version of this article says blockers do not work. I cannot write that, because mine did.

Four or five hours down to one, for months. Plenty of people use a blocker and never think about it again. If a hard limit works for you, keep it — nothing here is an argument for removing something that is doing its job.

And the honest thing to say about a pause is that it has far less evidence behind it than a blocker does. A pause could habituate. Interrupt somebody the same way a few hundred times and there is every reason to think it becomes scenery, the way a countdown does — which is a real risk and its own article.

A blocker fails in a specific way, for a reason the research describes, and the failure arrives at the worst possible moment. That is worth designing around.

## What I wanted instead

Years after all of that, my engagement ended, and I spent the worst of it scrolling — social media, games, the whole automatic repertoire. That is when I built something, and the Xiaomi experiment is the main reason I did not build a blocker.

I did not want another thing to beat. I already had enough of those.

What I wanted was the smallest possible interruption between the reflex and the app — enough to notice what I was doing, and no more. This is not a new idea, and it is not mine. When researchers stopped habitual popcorn eaters at a cinema, [what worked was not persuading them](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0146167211419863). It was making them eat with their non-dominant hand. The movement stopped being automatic, and the behaviour changed.

Nothing there involves winning an argument with yourself.

So, the tool I built does not block anything. It puts a few seconds and a sentence in front of the app, and then it gets out of the way. You can always continue. That is not a weakness in the design — it is the part that makes it survivable, because there is nothing to defeat and therefore no reason to uninstall it.

It feels strange the first few times. You open an app automatically, the pause appears, and you realise you did not want to be there. Then you close it. That small thing is most of what I was looking for.

It is not only social media, either. I put a pause on Clash of Clans, because I had built the same automatism there without noticing.

## The part that is still yours

I used to say I could leave these apps alone whenever I wanted. Then I tried, and found out that something built over months or years of repetition gets a say in what I do, whether or not I have given it permission.

A pause does not remove that. It just makes sure the reflex is not the only thing in the room.

I do not think the app saved me. It helped me save myself, which is a different claim and a much smaller one.

If a blocker is working for you, keep it. If you have uninstalled three of them, the problem was probably never your willpower.
